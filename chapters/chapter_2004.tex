\chapter{2004:Aaron Ciechanover and Avram Hershko and Irwin Rose}

\label{chap:chemistry2004}

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the year 2004 was awarded jointly to Aaron Ciechanover and Avram Hershko and Irwin Rose.

\textbf{Motivation:}

for the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation

\section{Aaron Ciechanover}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Aaron Ciechanover was born in 1947 in Haifa, Israel.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Ciechanover earned his M.D. from the Hebrew University-Hadassah Medical School in 1974 and his D.Sc. from the Technion-Israel Institute of Technology in 1982, where he is a professor.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Aaron Ciechanover was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2004 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation".

With Hershko and Rose he established that ubiquitin is covalently attached to proteins, marking them for degradation by the proteasome.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

Ubiquitin-mediated degradation controls the levels of most cellular proteins and is essential for cell division, DNA repair, and immunity.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Ciechanover continued his research at the Technion-Israel Institute of Technology. He shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\subsection*{Legacy}

The ubiquitin system is now known to regulate almost every cellular process.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Ciechanover's discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation revealed how cells tag proteins for destruction, a process that controls almost every cellular function, including the cell cycle, DNA repair, and immune responses. Ubiquitin biology is now the basis for the proteasome-inhibitor cancer drugs bortezomib and carfilzomib and for the protein-degradation approach called targeted protein degradation (PROTACs).

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Proposed Role of ATP in Protein Breakdown: Conjugation of Proteins with Multiple Chains of the Polypeptide of ATP-Dependent Proteolysis" (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 1980)

\section{Avram Hershko}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Avram Hershko was born in 1937 in Karcag, Hungary.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Hershko earned his M.D. and Ph.D. from the Hebrew University-Hadassah Medical School, and he is a professor at the Technion-Israel Institute of Technology.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Avram Hershko was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2004 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation".

He worked out the enzymatic cascade that conjugates ubiquitin to proteins, including the role of ATP in the reaction.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

His biochemical dissection identified the components of the ubiquitin system.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Hershko continued his research at the Technion-Israel Institute of Technology. He shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\subsection*{Legacy}

The reaction sequence he characterized is now a textbook chapter of cell biology.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Hershko's discovery of the ubiquitin proteolytic system, with Ciechanover and Rose, established the pathway by which cells destroy misfolded and short-lived proteins. This work explained how the cell cycle, gene expression, and immune responses are regulated, and it led to the development of proteasome inhibitor drugs used to treat multiple myeloma.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Proposed Role of ATP in Protein Breakdown: Conjugation of Proteins with Multiple Chains of the Polypeptide of ATP-Dependent Proteolysis" (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 1980)

\section{Irwin Rose}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Irwin Rose was born in 1926 in Brooklyn, New York, USA.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Rose earned his B.A. and Ph.D. from the University of Chicago (Ph.D., 1952). He worked at the Institute for Cancer Research in Philadelphia, and he later became a professor at the University of California, Irvine.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Irwin Rose was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2004 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation".

He contributed the biochemical analysis that identified the ATP-dependent conjugation of ubiquitin as the signal for protein breakdown.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The demonstration that ATP drives ubiquitin conjugation revealed how cells control protein lifetimes.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Rose moved to the University of California, Irvine, in 1997. He shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and he died in 2015.

\subsection*{Legacy}

His work helped establish the biochemistry of the ubiquitin pathway.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Rose's discovery that ubiquitin-dependent degradation requires ATP revealed the energetics of protein turnover and helped establish the ubiquitin-proteasome system as the cell's main protein destruction machinery. His work underpins modern research on protein quality control, neurodegeneration, and the development of PROTAC drugs.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Proposed Role of ATP in Protein Breakdown: Conjugation of Proteins with Multiple Chains of the Polypeptide of ATP-Dependent Proteolysis" (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 1980)

\section*{Chapter Summary}

The 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation. Ciechanover, Hershko, and Rose showed how cells tag proteins for destruction, a system now known to regulate nearly all cellular processes.
