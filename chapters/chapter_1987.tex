\chapter{1987:Donald J. Cram and Jean-Marie Lehn and Charles J. Pedersen}

\label{chap:chemistry1987}

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the year 1987 was awarded jointly to Donald J. Cram and Jean-Marie Lehn and Charles J. Pedersen.

\textbf{Motivation:}

for their development and use of molecules with structure-specific interactions of high selectivity

\section{Donald J. Cram}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Donald J. Cram was born in 1919 in Chester, Vermont, USA.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Cram studied at Rollins College (B.S., 1941) and the University of Nebraska (M.S., 1942), and he received his Ph.D. from the University of California, Los Angeles, in 1947. He spent his career at UCLA, where he worked on host-guest chemistry and molecular recognition.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Donald J. Cram was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1987 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their development and use of molecules with structure-specific interactions of high selectivity".

He built three-dimensional host molecules, such as spherands, with cavities that bind specific guest ions with high selectivity, and he formulated the principles of host-guest chemistry.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

Cram's synthetic hosts demonstrated that molecules could recognize and bind one another with enzyme-like selectivity, laying the foundation for molecular recognition research.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Cram remained at UCLA until his retirement, and he shared the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. He died on 2001-06-19.

\subsection*{Legacy}

The concept of the host-guest complex is now basic to supramolecular chemistry.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Cram's synthesis of molecules with designed three-dimensional structures, including spherands and cyclophanes, founded the field of host-guest chemistry and molecular recognition. His preorganization concept underpins the modern design of sensors, drug delivery systems, and self-assembling materials, and his work inspired the field of molecular machines.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Host-Guest Chemistry: Complexation between Simple Classes of Organic Compounds" (Science, 1988)

\clearpage

\section{Jean-Marie Lehn}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Jean-Marie Lehn was born in 1939 in Rosheim, France.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Lehn studied at the University of Strasbourg (Ph.D., 1963) and conducted postdoctoral work with Robert Burns Woodward at Harvard University. He was a professor at the University of Strasbourg from 1970 and at the Collège de France from 1980.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Jean-Marie Lehn was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1987 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their development and use of molecules with structure-specific interactions of high selectivity".

He synthesized cryptands, three-dimensional cage molecules with cavities that bind ions and small molecules with high selectivity, and he established the field he named supramolecular chemistry.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

Cryptands extended molecular recognition to three dimensions and helped create supramolecular chemistry as a discipline at the interface of chemistry and biology.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Lehn continued his research at the University of Strasbourg Institute of Advanced Study, and he shared the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\subsection*{Legacy}

His concepts of self-assembly and self-organization, together with his syntheses, define much of modern supramolecular chemistry.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Lehn's research on cryptates and supramolecular chemistry founded the field of supramolecular chemistry, where molecules assemble through non-covalent interactions. His work underpins the design of molecular sensors, drug delivery systems, self-healing materials, and the emerging field of molecular machines, and his concept of molecular information is a foundation of nanotechnology.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Diaza-Polyoxa-Macrocycles et Macrobicycles" (Tetrahedron Letters, 1969)

\clearpage

\section{Charles J. Pedersen}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Charles J. Pedersen was born in 1904 in Busan, Korea.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Pedersen studied at the University of Dayton and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (M.S., 1927), and he joined the DuPont Company in 1927, where he worked until 1969.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Charles J. Pedersen was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1987 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their development and use of molecules with structure-specific interactions of high selectivity".

He synthesized crown ethers, cyclic polyethers that selectively bind metal cations in their ring cavities, while seeking new catalysts at DuPont.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The first crown ethers, reported in 1967, showed that small synthetic macrocycles could mimic natural ion carriers, opening the field of macrocyclic host chemistry.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Pedersen retired from DuPont in 1969, and he shared the 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. He died on 1989-10-26.

\subsection*{Legacy}

Crown ethers are now widely used in synthesis, separation science, and materials chemistry.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Pedersen's discovery of crown ethers opened the field of macrocyclic host-guest chemistry and molecular recognition. Crown ethers are now used in phase-transfer catalysis, ion sensing, and the selective extraction of metal ions, and they provided the template for the development of cryptands, calixarenes, and modern supramolecular chemistry.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Cyclic Polyethers and Their Complexes with Metal Salts" (Journal of the American Chemical Society, 1967)

\clearpage

\section*{Chapter Summary}

The 1987 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized molecules that interact with one another with high selectivity. Pedersen discovered the crown ethers, Cram built refined host-guest systems, and Lehn created the cryptands and founded supramolecular chemistry.
