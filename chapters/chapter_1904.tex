\chapter{1904: Sir William Ramsay}
\label{chap:chemistry1904}

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the year 1904 was awarded to Sir William Ramsay.

\textbf{Motivation:}
in recognition of his services in the discovery of the inert gaseous elements in air, and his determination of their place in the periodic system

\section{Sir William Ramsay}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Sir William Ramsay was born on 1852-10-02 in Glasgow, Scotland.
Sir William Ramsay was a Scottish chemist who discovered the noble gases and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1904 "in recognition of his services in the discovery of the inert gaseous elements in air" along with his collaborator, John William Strutt, 3rd Baron Rayleigh, who received the Nobel Prize in Physics that same year for their discovery of argon. After the two men identified argon, Ramsay investigated other atmospheric gases. His work in isolating argon, helium, neon, krypton, and xenon led to the development of a new section of the periodic table.

Ramsay studied at the University of Glasgow and then in Germany under the organic chemist Rudolf Fittig at Tübingen, where he received his doctorate in 1872. After returning to Britain, he held academic posts at University College Bristol and, from 1887, at University College London, where he remained for the rest of his career. His early researches were in organic and physical chemistry, but his name will always be associated with the discovery of a whole family of elements.

\subsection*{Scientific Context}

The periodic table of Dmitri Mendeleev, published in 1869, had brought order to the known elements, but it contained a puzzling anomaly: there was no natural place for elements that were chemically inert, since all known elements combined with others to form compounds. The discovery of such elements was therefore completely unexpected, and it arose from a trivial-seeming discrepancy in the density of nitrogen. Lord Rayleigh, the Cavendish Professor of Physics at Cambridge, had found that nitrogen prepared from air appeared slightly denser than nitrogen prepared from chemical compounds, a difference too small to be explained by errors of measurement but too persistent to be ignored.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Ramsay was professor of chemistry at University College London for much of his career. Working with Lord Rayleigh, he isolated argon from air in 1894, and in 1895 he found helium in the mineral cleveite. With Morris Travers he then discovered neon, krypton, and xenon in liquid air, and in 1903 he identified helium produced by radioactive decay, completing a new group of the periodic table.

The collaboration between Ramsay and Rayleigh combined the skills of two very different investigators. Rayleigh had made the measurements that exposed the discrepancy in the density of nitrogen, and Ramsay took up the problem of isolating the unknown constituent of the air that accounted for it. By passing air repeatedly over heated magnesium, which absorbs nitrogen chemically, Ramsay obtained a residue of a gas that would not combine with anything, and which showed a characteristic spectrum. The two men announced the discovery of the new element argon in 1894, and Rayleigh received the credit in physics while Ramsay pursued the chemical side of the new field.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Sir William Ramsay was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1904 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "in recognition of his services in the discovery of the inert gaseous elements in air, and his determination of their place in the periodic system".

This research fundamentally advanced the field by discovering the noble gases (argon, helium, neon, krypton, xenon) and determining their place in the periodic system, completing a major gap in the periodic table.

The discovery of argon was followed by a series of finds that Ramsay and his collaborators made with extraordinary rapidity. In 1895 Ramsay found that the gas given off by the uranium mineral cleveite contained helium, the element that had been detected in the spectrum of the Sun in 1868 but never found on Earth. Then, using the newly available technique of liquefying air, Ramsay and Morris Travers separated the remaining inert gases by fractional distillation of liquid air, discovering neon, krypton, and xenon in quick succession in 1898. Finally, in 1903, Ramsay and Frederick Soddy showed that the gas helium is produced by the radioactive decay of radium, a result of great importance for the understanding of radioactivity.

\subsection*{The Work in Detail}

The isolation of argon depended on the discovery of a reagent that would remove every chemically active gas from air. Ramsay achieved this by passing air over red-hot magnesium, which combines with oxygen and nitrogen, and by treating the residue with hot copper oxide and with phosphorus, leaving only the inert gas. The density of the residual gas was then measured by weighing it in a glass globe of known volume, and the atomic weight was determined from the density by comparison with the behavior of other gases. The difficulty of such work in the 1890s, before the development of modern vacuum technique, can hardly be overestimated, and the accuracy of Ramsay's determinations was a measure of his experimental skill.

The discovery of helium on Earth, in 1895, was made in the mineral cleveite, which evolves gas when dissolved in acid. Ramsay heated the mineral and collected the evolved gas, which on examination in a spectroscope showed the characteristic yellow line of the solar element helium. This was the first demonstration that an element known from the spectrum of the Sun also exists on Earth, and it was a striking confirmation of the unity of the chemical elements throughout the universe.

\subsection*{Reception}

The discovery of the noble gases was received with astonishment by the chemical world, and the place of the new elements in the periodic system became the subject of intense discussion. Mendeleev had constructed his table on the assumption that all elements form compounds, and the existence of elements that formed none required a fundamental readjustment of chemical theory. Ramsay's solution was to add a new group, the zero group, at the edge of the periodic table, occupied by elements that were chemically inert. The discovery of the noble gases was thus not merely the isolation of new substances but a modification of the organizing principle of chemistry itself.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The impact of Sir William Ramsay's work extends far beyond the specific achievement recognized by the Nobel Prize.
The discovery of the noble gases completed the periodic table and led to the development of a new group of elements with unique properties, influencing both theoretical chemistry and practical applications in lighting and cryogenics.

The work of Ramsay and his collaborators also had a profound effect on the developing science of radioactivity. The discovery, made with Frederick Soddy, that helium is produced by the radioactive decay of radium provided strong evidence for the transmutation of elements and helped to establish the modern view that radioactivity involves the transformation of one element into another. This line of investigation, pursued at University College London, placed Ramsay at the center of the most exciting developments in physical science at the turn of the century.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Sir William Ramsay continued his scientific work until 1916-07-23, passing away in High Wycombe, United Kingdom.
He received numerous honors including membership in major scientific academies and societies.
Sir William Ramsay was knighted in 1902 and served as president of the Chemical Society and the British Association for the Advancement of Science.

Ramsay received honorary degrees from universities throughout Europe and America and was elected a foreign member of many national academies. He was appointed to the Order of Merit in 1902, and his laboratory at University College London attracted students and visitors from many countries. In his later years he devoted increasing attention to the study of radioactivity and to the practical applications of the noble gases, before his death in 1916.

\subsection*{Legacy}

The legacy of Sir William Ramsay endures through the lasting impact of his scientific contributions.
The noble gases, once considered chemically inert, now form compounds that have expanded the boundaries of chemistry. Sir William Ramsay's discovery completed a fundamental gap in the periodic system.

The noble-gas group—helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, and radon—now occupies a well-established place in the periodic table, and the term noble gases has passed into common speech. Ramsay discovered or helped establish the identities of all but radon, which was discovered by Friedrich Ernst Dorn in 1900. The discovery of the group had a significance that Ramsay himself could hardly have foreseen: determining the place of the noble gases in the periodic system contributed to the later understanding of atomic structure, since the inertness of these elements could be explained by the complete filling of their electronic shells. Ramsay thus stands at the beginning of the modern theory of the atom as well as at the end of classical chemistry.

\subsection*{Collaborators and Students}

Ramsay's collaborators included Lord Rayleigh, with whom he shared the discovery of argon, and Morris Travers, who worked with him on the separation of the rarer noble gases from liquid air. The young Frederick Soddy worked in Ramsay's laboratory and joined with him in the studies of radioactivity that led to the discovery of the production of helium from radium. Ramsay trained many students who went on to careers in chemistry throughout the British Empire, and his laboratory became one of the leading centers of inorganic and physical chemistry in the world.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Ramsay's isolation of the noble gases led to the modern understanding of the periodic table's final group and to practical applications ranging from neon lighting and argon-filled welding to helium-based cryogenics and MRI magnet cooling. Noble gas chemistry, long thought impossible, has since been demonstrated with compounds such as xenon fluorides. Industrial noble gas separation and gas-phase spectroscopy continue to depend on the methods Ramsay pioneered.

The inertness that made the noble gases so hard to discover is now understood as a consequence of the electron configuration of their atoms, and the demonstration in 1962 that xenon can be induced to form compounds opened a new chapter in noble gas chemistry. The industrial uses of the gases are ubiquitous: argon protects welds and incandescent lamps, neon glows in advertising signs and lasers, krypton fills energy-efficient windows, and helium is essential to cryogenic research and medical imaging. Each of these applications traces its origin to the work of Ramsay and his collaborators in the closing years of the nineteenth century.
