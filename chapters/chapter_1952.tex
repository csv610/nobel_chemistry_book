\chapter{1952:Archer J.P. Martin and Richard L.M. Synge}

\label{chap:chemistry1952}

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the year 1952 was awarded jointly to Archer J.P. Martin and Richard L.M. Synge.

\textbf{Motivation:}

for their invention of partition chromatography

\section{Archer J.P. Martin}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Archer J.P. Martin was born on 1910-03-01 in London, England.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Martin studied biochemistry at the University of Cambridge and obtained his doctorate there in 1936. He worked in the laboratories of the Wool Industries Research Association in Leeds, where he collaborated with Richard L.M. Synge. In 1941 they invented partition chromatography, separating amino acids between a moving solvent and a stationary water phase held on silica gel. Martin later developed gas-liquid chromatography with A.T.M. James in 1952.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Archer J.P. Martin was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1952 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their invention of partition chromatography".

Partition chromatography separated the components of a mixture on the basis of their differing distribution between two liquid phases, making it possible to separate and identify closely related substances such as the amino acids of a protein hydrolysate.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The method gave biochemists a practical way to separate the amino acids and other small molecules in complex mixtures, transforming the analysis of proteins and natural products. It became the ancestor of paper chromatography, gas-liquid chromatography, and the high-performance liquid chromatography used widely today.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Martin later worked at the National Institute for Medical Research in London and was a professor at the University of Cambridge. He died on 2002-07-28.

\subsection*{Legacy}

Partition chromatography and its successors became indispensable tools in chemistry, biochemistry, and clinical analysis, and Martin's name is linked with the chromatographic methods that dominate modern analytical chemistry.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Martin's partition chromatography, developed with Synge, founded modern separation science and made the development of insulin and other proteins possible. Chromatography is now the workhorse of pharmaceutical analysis, environmental testing, and the biotechnology industry, and paper chromatography became the basis for early genome sequencing efforts.

\section{Richard L.M. Synge}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Richard L.M. Synge was born on 1914-10-28 in Liverpool, England.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Synge studied at Winchester College and Trinity College, Cambridge, and took his doctorate at Cambridge in 1941. He worked at the Wool Industries Research Association in Leeds, where he and Archer J.P. Martin developed partition chromatography for the separation of amino acids. He later worked at the Rowett Research Institute in Aberdeen, where he applied the method to peptides and other biological materials.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Richard L.M. Synge was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1952 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their invention of partition chromatography".

Synge's theoretical and experimental work with Martin turned the idea of a partition-based separation into a working chromatographic method, and his application of it to the study of peptides helped establish protein chemistry on a sound analytical basis.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The method provided a rapid, sensitive means of analyzing mixtures of amino acids and peptides, essential to the later determination of protein structure. Its influence extended into medicine, where chromatographic methods became standard for the analysis of biological fluids.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Synge moved to the Food Research Institute in Norwich and remained active in carbohydrate chemistry and protein analysis. He died on 1994-08-18.

\subsection*{Legacy}

Synge's work with Martin founded modern chromatographic analysis, and his contributions are remembered in the widespread laboratory use of partition and related chromatographic techniques.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

The Synge–Martin paper chromatography technique transformed biochemical analysis and made possible the identification of amino acids, sugars, and nucleotides in complex mixtures. Chromatography in all its modern forms, including HPLC and gas chromatography, descends from this foundational work and is used in every pharmaceutical, clinical, and environmental laboratory.

\section*{Chapter Summary}

The 1952 prize recognized the invention of partition chromatography. Archer J.P. Martin and Richard L.M. Synge devised the method in 1941 while working in Leeds, using the differing distribution of substances between two liquid phases to separate amino acids and other components of complex mixtures.
