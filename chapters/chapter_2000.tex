\chapter{2000:Alan Heeger and Alan MacDiarmid and Hideki Shirakawa}

\label{chap:chemistry2000}

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the year 2000 was awarded jointly to Alan Heeger and Alan MacDiarmid and Hideki Shirakawa.

\textbf{Motivation:}

for the discovery and development of conductive polymers

\section{Alan Heeger}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Alan Heeger was born in 1936 in Sioux City, Iowa, USA.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Heeger studied at the University of Nebraska (B.S., 1957) and earned his Ph.D. from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1961. He taught at the University of Pennsylvania before joining the University of California, Santa Barbara, in 1982.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Alan Heeger was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2000 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for the discovery and development of conductive polymers".

With MacDiarmid and Shirakawa he discovered that doping the polymer polyacetylene with iodine increases its electrical conductivity dramatically, by up to a million-fold.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The discovery created the field of conducting polymers and organic electronic materials.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Heeger continued his research at the University of California, Santa Barbara, where he is professor emeritus. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\subsection*{Legacy}

Conductive polymers are now used in organic light-emitting diodes, solar cells, and other electronic devices.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Electrical Conductivity in Doped Polyacetylene" (Physical Review Letters, 1977)

\clearpage

\section{Alan MacDiarmid}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Alan MacDiarmid was born in 1927 in Masterton, New Zealand.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

MacDiarmid studied at the University of New Zealand (B.Sc., M.Sc.) and earned his Ph.D. from the University of Cambridge in 1955. He joined the University of Pennsylvania in 1955, where he spent his career.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Alan MacDiarmid was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2000 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for the discovery and development of conductive polymers".

He and his colleagues oxidized polyacetylene with halogen vapors, producing the first electrically conducting organic polymer.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The demonstration that a plastic could conduct electricity overturned the assumption that polymers were insulators.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

MacDiarmid remained at the University of Pennsylvania. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and he died in 2007.

\subsection*{Legacy}

His work established the chemistry of conducting organic polymers.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Electrical Conductivity in Doped Polyacetylene" (Physical Review Letters, 1977)

\clearpage

\section{Hideki Shirakawa}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Hideki Shirakawa was born in 1936 in Tokyo, Japan.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Shirakawa studied at the Tokyo Institute of Technology (B.S., 1961; Ph.D., 1966). He was a professor at the University of Tsukuba from 1979.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Hideki Shirakawa was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2000 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for the discovery and development of conductive polymers".

He developed the method for preparing polyacetylene as silvery films, the material whose iodination produced the first conducting polymer.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

His reproducible synthesis of polyacetylene films made the discovery of polymer conductivity possible.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Shirakawa continued his research at the University of Tsukuba. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\subsection*{Legacy}

His polymerization methods remain fundamental to conducting polymer chemistry.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Electrical Conductivity in Doped Polyacetylene" (Physical Review Letters, 1977)

\clearpage

\section*{Chapter Summary}

The 2000 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized the discovery that polymers can be made electrically conductive by doping. Heeger, MacDiarmid, and Shirakawa's work on doped polyacetylene founded the science of conducting polymers.
