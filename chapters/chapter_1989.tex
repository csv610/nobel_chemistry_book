\chapter{1989:Sidney Altman and Thomas R. Cech}

\label{chap:chemistry1989}

The Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the year 1989 was awarded jointly to Sidney Altman and Thomas R. Cech.

\textbf{Motivation:}

for their discovery of catalytic properties of RNA

\section{Sidney Altman}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Sidney Altman was born in 1939 in Montreal, Canada.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Altman studied at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (B.Sc., 1960) and earned his Ph.D. from the University of Colorado in 1967. He joined Yale University in 1971, where he spent his career.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Sidney Altman was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1989 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their discovery of catalytic properties of RNA".

He discovered that the RNA component of ribonuclease P, not its protein part, is the catalytic subunit that processes transfer RNA precursors.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

The finding that RNA itself is the catalyst overturned the long-held assumption that only proteins could be enzymes.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Altman remained at Yale University, where he served as chairman of the biology department. He shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and he died in 2022.

\subsection*{Legacy}

The discovery of catalytic RNA supported the idea of an early RNA world and founded the field of ribozyme research.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Altman's discovery that RNA can act as a catalyst, together with Cech's, established the RNA world hypothesis and founded the field of ribozyme research. Catalytic RNA is now used in biotechnology and medicine, and the concept underpins our understanding of the origins of life and the design of ribozyme-based tools and therapeutics.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Ribonuclease P: An Enzyme with an Essential RNA Component" (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 1978)

\section{Thomas R. Cech}

\subsection*{Early Life and Education}

Thomas R. Cech was born in 1947 in Chicago, Illinois, USA.

\subsection*{Career and Major Research}

Cech studied at Grinnell College (B.A., 1970) and earned his Ph.D. from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1975. He joined the University of Colorado Boulder in 1978.

\subsection*{Nobel Prize-winning Work}

The work for which Thomas R. Cech was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1989 was described by the Nobel Committee as: "for their discovery of catalytic properties of RNA".

He discovered that the ribosomal RNA precursor of the ciliate Tetrahymena thermophila removes its own intron without any protein, showing that RNA can catalyze its own splicing.

\subsection*{Significance and Impact}

Self-splicing RNA demonstrated RNA catalysis directly and had broad implications for understanding early evolution.

\subsection*{Later Career and Honors}

Cech remained at the University of Colorado Boulder, and he served as president of the Howard Hughes Medical Institute from 2000 to 2008. He shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

\subsection*{Legacy}

The ribozymes he characterized became tools for biochemistry and inspired the RNA world hypothesis.

\subsection*{Modern Developments}

Cech's discovery of catalytic RNA (ribozymes) in Tetrahymena revealed that RNA is not merely a carrier of genetic information but a biological catalyst. This discovery reshaped the understanding of the origins of life, supported the RNA world hypothesis, and founded the field of RNA enzymology, which now informs RNA-based therapeutics and genome-editing tools.

\subsection*{Selected Publications}

"Self-splicing RNA: Autoexcision and Autocyclization of the Ribosomal RNA Intervening Sequence of Tetrahymena" (Cell, 1982)

\clearpage

\section*{Chapter Summary}

The 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized the discovery that RNA can act as a catalyst. Altman and Cech independently showed that RNA possesses enzymatic activity, a finding that reshaped molecular biology and theories of early life.
